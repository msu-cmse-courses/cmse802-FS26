# Day 2 Instructor Notes - Projects

## Goals for the Day

Day 2 is focused on helping students connect the course to their own research. The primary objective is to begin scoping semester projects while introducing the concept of modeling as a framework for understanding research problems.

Key messages to communicate:

- The semester project should directly support the student's research whenever possible.
- The course is not about completing a prescribed modeling project. Instead, students should apply research software engineering practices to their own work.
- Students should begin thinking about how software can advance, automate, document, or improve their research process.
- Understanding the research interests of other students is valuable because many projects share common challenges, even when the research domains differ.

## Activity: Project and Modeling Discussions

Most of the class period is devoted to discussion.

Students should be encouraged to:

- Introduce their research area.
- Describe their current project or research goals.
- Share how computation, data, simulation, or software fits into their work.
- Ask questions about each other's projects.
- Identify common challenges across projects.

The instructor should facilitate discussion and help students refine project ideas into something appropriate for the course.

A useful guiding question is:

> What software will you build, improve, or maintain this semester that will advance your research?

## Modeling Framework

Introduce the three aspects of a model:

- Analytical Models
- Physical Models
- Data Models

The goal is not to force every project into a single category. Most research projects involve multiple forms of modeling.

Students should begin thinking about questions such as:

- Is this primarily a simulation?
- Is this primarily a data analysis problem?
- Is this primarily a mathematical or analytical model?
- How do these different modeling approaches interact within the research workflow?

The modeling discussion provides a common language that allows students from very different disciplines to discuss their research and identify similarities.

## Scoping Student Projects

One of the instructor's most important roles on Day 2 is helping students define projects that fit the goals of the course.

Topics that may emerge include:

- Simulation and computational modeling
- Data analysis and visualization
- Machine learning or regression
- Data collection and data wrangling
- Workflow automation
- High-performance computing
- Reproducible research pipelines
- Scientific software development

The exact technical domain is less important than ensuring the project provides opportunities for meaningful software development and improvement throughout the semester.

## Teaching Philosophy

Students often assume their project must satisfy a specific modeling requirement. This is generally not the case.

The primary goal is for students to write and improve software that supports their own research.

The software should become more:

- Robust
- Reproducible
- Safe
- Literate
- Portable

These qualities provide opportunities to introduce new tools and practices throughout the semester.

As students learn topics such as version control, testing, documentation, packaging, environments, automation, reproducibility, and collaboration, they should have opportunities to apply those ideas directly to their own research software.

This approach allows course content to immediately reinforce the student's ongoing research activities.

## What to Expect

Many students will arrive with projects that are either too large or too vague.

Common challenges include:

- A project that is essentially an entire dissertation.
- A project that focuses primarily on research results rather than software.
- A project that is not sufficiently defined.
- Students who are unsure how software contributes to their research.
- Students who have not yet identified a research project.

The instructor should help students focus on a manageable software component that can be improved throughout the semester.

## Suggested Strategy

Allow substantial time for discussion.

Students often learn as much from hearing about other projects as they do from presenting their own.

Consider asking students to identify:

- Similarities between projects.
- Shared software challenges.
- Common data-management issues.
- Opportunities for collaboration or knowledge sharing.

The discussion helps build a learning community while reinforcing that many research software challenges are shared across disciplines.

## Warning Signs

Follow up with students who:

- Cannot clearly identify a research project.
- Propose projects focused entirely on generating research results instead of developing software.
- Have projects that are too broad for a single semester.
- Struggle to explain how software fits into their research workflow.
- Remain disengaged from discussions about other student projects.

These concerns are easiest to address early in the semester before students become heavily invested in a project direction.

## End of Class Checkpoint

By the end of Day 2, students should ideally:

- Have identified a semester project.
- Understand the relationship between their research and software development.
- Be familiar with the concepts of analytical, physical, and data models.
- Have shared their project idea with the class.
- Have received feedback on project scope.
- Have begun identifying software engineering improvements they can make throughout the semester.

The most important outcome is that students leave class with a clearer understanding of how their research project will serve as the foundation for applying research software engineering practices throughout the semester.
