## 3. Is this the right course for you?

This course will use the Python programming language extensively and you should have a programming skill level similar to students finishing courses such as CMSE 201, CMSE 801, or equivalent. You should be familiar with defining variables, basic mathematical operations, control loops, conditional and logical statements, input/output, and error handling using Python. ***If not, you will not have sufficient background and should take CMSE 801 instead***.

On the other end of the spectrum, this course aims to introduce a number of basic concepts in computational modeling.
It is mostly a survey course of commonly used approaches, with little time to explore each approach in depth.
***If you already have the background in most of the topics introduced below, this course might not be at your level***. You may consider special topic courses offered by CMSE, CSE, STT, or MTH.

## 4. Synopsis

### Course goal and teaching philosophy

CMSE 802 is a research software course for graduate students who need to build software that advances real research goals. The course is designed as a software-writing seminar: you make steady progress on your own research-aligned project while practicing professional engineering habits.

Our quality framework is:

- Safe
- Portable
- Reproducible
- Robust
- Literate

These five dimensions are used throughout class activities, peer review, project checkpoints, and final evaluation.

### AI in this course

AI-assisted development is encouraged, but outputs must be validated. Treat AI as a collaborator to evaluate, not an oracle to obey.

When you use AI tools, you are expected to:

- verify claims with tests, reasoning, or direct checks
- document what AI contributed and what you changed
- cite substantial AI-generated content when required by assignment instructions

### Learning objectives

By the end of this course, you should be able to:

- Build research software that is usable beyond one-off scripts.
- Apply sustainable software engineering workflows to your own research code.
- Use AI assistants responsibly with explicit validation and trust calibration.
- Explain how Analytical, Physical, and Data-Driven components combine in models connected to your research questions.
- Deliver a final software project with clear evidence of software quality and reproducibility.

### Primary topics

- Research software engineering workflows (version control, modular design, testing, documentation, review).
- Conceptual modeling fluency using Analytical, Physical, and Data-Driven components as a working convention to guide discussion and design choices.
- Reproducibility practices (environment strategy, run instructions, evidence capture).
- Collaborative development and handoff quality.

## 5. Course format

### How class time is used

Most class meetings combine short framing lectures with guided project work, technical studios, and peer feedback. The emphasis is practical: each week should produce visible progress in your repository.

### Course components and assessment

**In-class work and participation:** Active engagement in class coding, team collaboration, and discussions.

**Project checkpoints:** Short, evidence-based submissions throughout the semester. Typical evidence includes repository links, tests, reproducibility notes, and concise reflections.

**Peer reproducibility review:** Structured review using the course SPRRL rubric/checklists. You will both review peers and respond to feedback on your own project.

**Final project and presentation:** A research-aligned software artifact plus a final presentation that explains both scientific value and engineering quality.

**Midterm exam:** This course does not include a midterm exam.

### Academic honesty

Intellectual integrity is the foundation of scientific enterprise. In all instances, you must do your own work and give proper credit to all sources that you use in your papers and oral presentations -- any instance of submitting another person's work, ideas, or wording as your own counts as plagiarism. This includes failing to cite any direct quotations in your essays, research paper, class debate, or written presentation. The MSU College of Engineering adheres to the policies of academic honesty as specified in the General Student Regulations 1.0, Protection of Scholarship and Grades, and in the all-University statement on Integrity of Scholarship and Grades, which are included in Spartan Life: Student Handbook and Resource Guide. Students who plagiarize will receive a 0.0 in the course. In addition, University policy requires that any cheating offense, regardless of the magnitude of the infraction or punishment decided upon by the professor, be reported immediately to the dean of the student's college. (See also the [Academic Integrity](https://www.msu.edu/~ombud/academic-integrity/index.html) webpage.)

It is important to note that plagiarism in the context of this course includes, but is not limited to, directly copying another student's solutions to assignments; copying materials from online sources, textbooks, or other reference materials without citing those references in your source code or documentation or having somebody else do your in-class work or homework on your behalf. Any work that is done in collaboration with other students should state this explicitly and have their names as well as yours listed clearly.

More broadly, we ask that students adhere to the Spartan Code of Honor academic pledge, as written by the Associated Students of Michigan State University (ASMSU):

"As a Spartan, I will strive to uphold values of the highest ethical standard. I will practice honesty in my work, foster honesty in my peers, and take pride in knowing that honor is worth more than grades. I will carry these values beyond my time as a student at Michigan State University, continuing the endeavor to build personal integrity in all that I do."
